Chapter VI
Moving Forward
The focus of this report has been on the processes of designing the NID, its legislative measures, implementation practices, and citizens’ concerns. Body & Data found that Nepal’s National ID program reflects an ambitious effort to improve governance and improve access to public services through digital systems. However, as this report has shown, the process remains deeply flawed in its current form. Weak governance structures, inadequate consultation with stakeholders, insufficient transparency, and poor digital infrastructure have undermined the program’s stated goals. Particularly concerning are the risks to privacy, dignity, and safety of marginalized groups, including women, people with disabilities, and queer communities, who face systemic exclusion and heightened vulnerabilities under the current design.
The absence of a comprehensive data protection law and the reliance on centralized biometric databases raise pressing questions about security, accountability, and human rights. Without robust safeguards, the promise of efficiency and inclusion risks being overshadowed by the reality of surveillance, coercion, and discrimination.
Moving forward, the success of the NID system will depend on the government’s willingness to center rights, transparency, and meaningful participation in its approach. Inclusive consultation, stronger legal protections, and an unwavering commitment to equity are essential to ensure that the NID becomes a tool for good governance and empowerment rather than surveillance and exclusion.
Building on the previous work and recommendations by Body & Data (see Box 12), the report proposes a range of recommendations, from broad to specific, for the government, private sectors, and civil society organizations.
Box 12: Recommendations from Digitization of Identity in Nepal: Efforts, Experiences, and Effects
Based on the 2021 pilot study on the NID, Digitization of Identity in Nepal: Efforts, Experiences, and Effects, Body & Data had made recommendations directed at Nepal’s policymakers, civil society organizations, and researchers. As the NID system continues to expand, it is important to revisit and reinforce these recommendations in light of growing concerns over data governance, inclusion, and human rights.
We emphasized that Nepal’s policymakers should acknowledge that mandatory national digital ID systems collecting excessive amounts of personal demographic and biometric data from citizens must have robust governance and accountability protocols in place to protect the human rights of individuals, especially those from marginalized communities.
Along with strict governance standards, the government must adopt a strategic communication policy that credibly and completely informs all aspects of the program to prevent misinformation. Biometric-based digital ID systems often reinforce deep-rooted socio-cultural exclusions, disproportionately affecting marginalized communities. Therefore, we recommend that no individual be denied access to public services based on possession of a digital ID.

Recommendations for the Government
While official rhetoric frames the National ID as a driver of administrative efficiency and digital inclusion, our findings indicate that these goals are severely compromised by poor governance and fragmented implementation. The rollout demonstrates a marked absence of inclusive design, operational transparency, and systemic accountability.
To resolve these structural flaws, we present policy recommendations organized around three core pillars: Governance, Accessibility, and Accountability. Urgent reform across these domains is essential if Nepal is to construct a digital identity ecosystem that is secure, just, and truly beneficial to all citizens.
Transition from compulsory rollout to consent-based adoption. To fulfill its mandate as an administrative tool for public convenience, the NID must be implemented on a voluntary basis. Forcing registration by conditioning access to essential state services creates administrative coercion. The government must adopt a human-centered approach that relies on informed consent, clear public education, and demonstrated service efficiency to build organic trust and user adoption.
Ensure a universal and non-discretionary access. Designed as a digital service foundation rather than a proof of citizenship, the NID should theoretically lower barriers to public services. In practice, however, requiring a citizenship certificate for enrollment automatically locks millions of vulnerable residents out of essential digital services. The government must align policy with intent by making the NID accessible to all residents. Furthermore, physical and linguistic barriers must be dismantled by equipping enrollment stations with disability-accessible facilities, local language support, sign language interpretation, and accessible media.
Build a robust digital public infrastructure (DPI). A functional DPI cannot be realized through administrative decree alone. Fulfilling the promise of “smart” governance requires parallel investments in reliable network connectivity, secure cross-system interoperability, and technical capacity-building within the civil service. Mandating digital identity adoption without establishing these prerequisite foundations endangers security and frustrates public access.
Enact strong legal and policy frameworks. The current statutory basis, the National Identity Card and Civil Registration Act 2020, offers insufficient safeguards for mass biometric data collection. Before expanding NID integration across state and private platforms, Nepal must pass comprehensive data protection and privacy laws. These legislative measures must explicitly define data use boundaries, enforce informed consent, outlaw surveillance-oriented practices, and institute strict liability for data misuse.
Implement ethical and secure data governance. Managing sensitive personal and biometric information demands strict adherence to ethical data principles. The government must codify purpose-limitation rules, require explicit user consent before any cross-departmental data transfer, and implement rigorous access controls and encryption standards. Inter-agency data sharing – including for voter rolls, civil registration, and social protection – must be narrowly defined by law and subjected to independent oversight to prevent unauthorized access and function creep.
Ensure secure interoperability, inter-agency coordination, and institutional clarity. A secure DPI relies on integrated interoperable systems that enable real-time identity verification and service delivery across government agencies. To resolve the current state of administrative fragmentation, the state must establish a clear regulatory body governing data authority and technical protocols. Interoperability systems must embed privacy-by-design principles, ensuring that data exchange streamlines public service delivery without creating centralized mechanisms for citizen surveillance.
Develop capable, dignified and culturally-literate public workforce. Public sector personnel require both technical training and human-centered professional development. State institutions must institute mandatory capacity-building programs covering ICT tools, data privacy, and security protocols. Crucially, addressing frontline abuses against marginalized groups requires targeted training in anti-bias, empathetic communication, and inclusive service delivery, ensuring that administrative processes respect the dignity of all citizens.
Strengthen digital literacy and embed digital rights education. Expanding digital literacy requires a long-term strategy that moves beyond transactional technical training. Curriculum development across primary, secondary, and community education platforms must integrate critical digital literacy, focused on privacy rights, misinformation, and digital safety, alongside basic operational skills. To ensure equitable uptake, these educational initiatives must be coupled with physical infrastructure upgrades, offline technical support, and accessible materials in local languages. Targeted programs must prioritize women and historically excluded communities, drawing on cross-sectoral partnerships with civil society and media networks to maximize reach and sustain long-term engagement.
Foster public transparency and consultative governance. The top-down, non-transparent deployment of the NID undermines public trust. The government must make system architecture documentation, procurement agreements, and third-party vendor contracts, especially those with foreign entities, publicly accessible. Furthermore, implementation must transition from an insular administrative process to a participatory dialogue that incorporates civil society, digital rights experts, and vulnerable communities. Routine public disclosures regarding data governance, system vulnerability audits, and security breaches must be statutorily required.
Establish independent oversight and grievance mechanisms. To safeguard against systemic abuse and operational failures, Nepal must establish an autonomous regulatory body to oversee NID operations. This body should oversee mandatory periodic audits of data infrastructure and operational compliance, making all reports public. To ensure accountability, the legal framework must enforce strict liabilities for data breaches or unauthorized access, alongside accessible, transparent grievance redress mechanisms for affected citizens.
Recommendations for the Private Sector
In Nepal, the private sector has played a commendable role in laying the groundwork for digital public infrastructure (DPI), including proactive efforts in advancing digital literacy programs. However, there have also been instances where private actors have avoided accountability, undermining trust and rights-based practices.
Given that the NID, digital governance, and digital service delivery are still in their early stages, private companies must play a constructive role.
Uphold ethical data practices. Businesses using NID systems must enforce strong data minimization principles, clear privacy policies in local languages, and robust breach response protocols. Companies must guarantee that NID-based verification remains optional, offering alternative identification methods so that citizens are not denied service access. Furthermore, corporate entities must forbid the use of NID data for commercial profiling, monetization, or tracking, and reject state requests for unauthorized access or surveillance backdoors.
Design inclusive services and responsible innovation. Commercial platforms connected to the NID should be designed for universal access, catering to marginalized communities and persons with disabilities. Private firms must avoid implementing high-risk biometric technologies without proven safeguards, clear auditability, and legal liability mechanisms.
Contribute to public awareness and multi-stakeholders collaboration. Private sector actors should utilize their customer onboarding platforms to educate the public on digital rights, privacy settings, and data protection. Furthermore, businesses must collaborate with state agencies, civil society organizations, and policy experts to develop open technical standards and co-create ethical AI and data governance frameworks.
Recommendations for Civil Society
Civil society and varieties of its organizations occupy an indispensable position in shaping rights-based digital governance, serving as vital bulwarks to ensure that the NID system neither deepens systemic exclusion or compromises fundamental privacy. As the NID system expands, civil society actors must actively function as vigilant watchdogs, vocal advocates, and catalysts of public understanding.
CSOs in particular should work alongside vulnerable groups to ensure that the NID system rigorously upholds the principles of data privacy, informed consent, and non-discrimination. This mandate includes holding state agencies and private actors accountable for the lifecycle of personal and biometric data collected and storage, and engaging in legal and policy advocacy to embed privacy and human rights at the foundation of digital identity governance
Advocate for rights-based legal and policy frameworks. Civil society broadly and CSOs in particular, must advocate comprehensive technological policies that prioritize privacy and rigorous data protection while upholding human dignity and equity in digital governance. This imperatives entails advancing data protection legislation anchored in international standards, promoting safeguards against state surveillance, behavioral profiling, and algorithmic discrimination, and ensuring that digital identity never serves as a mandatory barrier to essential public services.
Strengthen digital literacy and digital rights education. CSOs can serve as vital partners alongside the state, empowering communities, especially historically marginalized groups, through digital literacy initiatives. These programs should explicitly integrate digital rights education, ensuring that citizens not only acquire the technical skills required to navigate digital systems but also cultivate the critical agency necessary to hold the government and service providers accountable. Furthermore, these efforts must demystify the institutional implications of linking the NID system to basic welfare services while providing transparent, accessible pathways to legal recourse for those who experience administrative exclusion.
Facilitate evidence-based public discussions and center lived experiences. CSOs should lead informed dialogue concerning the multifaceted risks and opportunities of digital ID systems by organizing public forums, media engagements, and cross-disciplinary academic collaborations . These discussions must prioritize and amplify the lived experiences of marginalized individuals who face structural barriers to digital enrollment or suffer tangible harm from digital exclusion.
Monitor implementation and report harms. Civil society must function as independent watchdogs to ensure that the rollout of the National ID system adheres strictly to the principles of equity, accountability, and human rights. Through social audits and community-based monitoring, they can identify and document systemic patterns of exclusion, discrimination, or invasive state surveillance.
Build coalitions and cross-sector partnerships. To effectively navigate the complex, deeply intertwined impacts of the National ID system, CSOs must forge strong coalitions and collaborate with media, technologists, lawyers, researchers, and digital rights networks. It is imperative to foster active regional and international partnerships to harmonize advocacy strategies, share cross-border insights, and champion digital rights amid a rapidly shifting and increasingly urgent global landscape.
