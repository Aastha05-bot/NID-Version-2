Executive Summary & Core Research Questions
In November 2018, the Government of Nepal launched its National ID (NID) system, an ambitious digital identity framework aimed at achieving universal biometric enrollment of all residents by 2023. Under this scheme, each individual is assigned a unique 10-digit identification number anchored to biometric indicators such as digital photographs, ten-fingerprints and iris scans, alongside core demographic data. Designed primarily as an online verification mechanism to streamline governance and service delivery, the NID’s functional application within the digital ecosystem has, however, remained profoundly underutilized since its inception.
This study evaluates the socio-political implications and ground-level realities of the National ID along three primary vectors: public understanding of biometric identity, the underlying political imperatives of the Nepali state digital transformation agenda, and the lived experiences of the citizens during enrollment. To that end, the scope of the inquiry is structured around three core questions:
Question 01 — Why is the Nepali state prioritizing digital identification?
Addressed in Chapter II, which traces the policy origins and intended trajectory of the initiative.
Question 02 — How is the government executing its digital identification scheme?
Addressed in Chapter III, which examines the evolving political ambitions of the Nepali state against the material hurdles of execution.
Question 03 — How do citizens experience and navigate the government’s ambitious biometric initiative?
Addressed in Chapters IV and V, which evaluate the governance conditions shaping citizens’ access, the politics of rights and entitlement, civil society engagement, and the fault lines between inclusion and surveillance.
Because operational adoption of the NID remains stalled by a lack of card-reading machines across government offices, this analysis centers on the overarching design, governance, and enrollment workflows. The study expands upon earlier empirical work conducted by Body & Data during the project’s initial pilot phase.
Mandatory enrollment in the National ID system grew rapidly from 16 million in late 2024 to over 20 million by mid-2026. Card distribution has gained significant momentum since its 2022 rollout, with over 7.7 millions physical cards forwarded to District Administration Offices (DAOs). Although designed purely as a digital authentication mechanism rather than a citizenship certificate, access to the NID is nevertheless restricted to citizens holding formal documentation. Consequently, millions of historically vulnerable individuals, including the landless, marginalized groups, and children of single mothers unable to obtain citizenship, are excluded from the digital system.
Nepal’s push for the NID is anchored in the expectation that digital solutions will enhance governance efficiency, cross-sectoral interoperability, and public service accessibility. The system is designed to function as a standardized digital ID spanning both state and private enterprises. Notably, the system is not currently utilized for welfare benefit delivery. Rather, its overarching mandate is to rationalize Nepal’s fragmented identification system by integrating existing credentials, including citizenship cards, driving licenses, voter registration, and Below Poverty Line (BPL) cards, into a single digital platform.
A critical gap persists between official policy commitments and their tangible delivery on the ground. Unfolding through an opaque, top-down strategy, Nepal’s NID implementation reflects minimal public participation and limited institutional foresight. The state’s push toward ‘smart’ governance remains largely speculative, unbacked by the necessary capital and regulatory commitments to build strong digital public infrastructure. Without targeted investments in technology adoption, robust data governance, and digital inclusion, Nepal risks cementing a fragile infrastructure alongside an alienated public.
In the absence of a systematic effort to retrain and reorient civil servants toward digital transformation, conventional administrative practices continue to prevail. This institutional bottleneck is further exacerbated by insufficient education and capacity-building initiatives focused on information and communications technology (ICT) and biometric technologies. Overall, the implementation strategy has lacked meaningful public outreach and educational frameworks, particularly with respect to raising digital literacy and mitigating privacy and data protection risks.
Public feedback underscores that mandatory NID registration represents a superfluous administrative layer that forces citizens to obtain an additional identifier without clear benefits. While official rhetoric promises the integration of social security allowances into the National ID system, these welfare enhancements remain unrealized. Consequently, citizens bear the weight of added bureaucracy while receiving no immediate material returns. From a governance perspective, this emphasis on population tracking absent corresponding welfare delivery indicates a strategic bias toward surveillance and state control over citizen empowerment and inclusive social protection.
The NID rollout revealed a distinct lack of federal-local coordination, with the central government bypassing substantive intergovernmental dialogue. Local authorities and District Administration Offices were positioned as passive operational actors rather than strategic partners, receiving only basic technical instructions on machine operation and data gathering. Lacking a comprehensive understanding of the NID’s digital framework, municipal officials were unable to guide their constituents. This systemic communication breakdown left citizens poorly informed about the system’s utility and largely unaware of biometric data governance and privacy rights.
Although the NID card is now embedded within routine administrative procedures, Nepal’s shift toward “smart” governance suffers from structural ad-hocism and a deficit of strategic direction. Entrenched in traditional bureaucratic inertia, implementation continues to stumble over poor digital infrastructure, unreliable internet access, and recurring grid failures. These operational friction points fall heaviest on women and historically marginalized groups. Moreover, a critical gap persists between rhetoric and reality: while existing guidelines nominally acknowledge accessibility barriers for persons with disabilities, day-to-day administrative practices remain exclusionary.
As digital rights and literacy remain relatively new areas of focus for civil society and private sector organizations, understanding of the National ID system’s biometric footprint remains limited. This knowledge gap was further compounded by the state’s failure to consult non-governmental stakeholders during the project’s design and rollout. The complete absence of substantive public debate on biometric data management highlights a vital function for digital rights advocates: holding governments and international development partners accountable. Furthermore, this dynamic reveals a sharp divergence between the high-minded rhetoric of digital governance and the exclusionary realities of its implementation.
